Part 4 · Chapter 13

Normalising and weighting

You now have many utilities, each on its own scale. Chapter 13 is about making them comparable and merging them into one number. This chapter is the direct ancestor of the Infinite Axis system.

Choosing a scale

The book opens with a recording-studio story it calls climbing fader syndrome. Every musician asks for their part to be a bit louder. After an hour every fader is at the top and nothing is louder than anything else. Scores do the same thing if every factor is allowed to be "the most important".

There are three ways to state a score, and you should know which one you are using.

Which one to use

Inputs to a decision: relative to a maximum (that is normalisation, below). Choosing among final scores: relative to each other (that is the weighted random selection in Part 5).

Granularity

How many distinct values does a scale need? The book uses the medical pain scale, which runs 0 to 10. A 0 to 100 scale would claim a precision nobody has: no patient can tell a 62 from a 63. A 0 to 3 scale could not separate cases that need different treatment.

The rule: enough steps to tell apart the situations that should lead to different behaviour, and no more. If your agent has five visible reactions, a factor measured to six decimal places is not buying you anything.

Normalising

Normalising rescales a value into a standard range, almost always 0 to 1. Once everything is 0 to 1 you can compare a distance with a health value with an ammo count.

normalised = (value − min) / (max − min)    then clamp to 0..1
From the book: "How Much Weight?"

An RPG character carries 52 pounds and can carry at most some limit. Dividing gives burden, a number from 0 to 1. Movement speed, fatigue and whether the character can jump can all read burden without knowing the pounds or the limit. A weak character and a strong one share the same rules.

Nonlinear normalisation

The scale does not have to be straight. The book's "Are We There Yet?" example takes the marginal utility of building units from Part 2 and normalises the progress: how far along am I toward the number I want? The curve then has the same shape whether the goal is 5 units or 50.

utility of the next unit = 1 − (count / goal)^k
From the book: "Who's Next?"

Workers and soldiers each get that formula. The book gives workers the exponent 2 so their curve is flatter than the soldiers' curve. Because both results are on a 0 to 1 scale, deciding what to build is one comparison: build whichever has the higher utility. No rule mentions either goal directly.

Build order from two normalised curves

Press Build repeatedly. The agent always builds the unit whose next copy has the higher utility. The soldier exponent is my choice (the book only fixes the worker's at 2).

Change one goal to 20 and build again. The order adapts and you did not touch any logic. That is why the book calls normalised utility a way to turn moving targets into fixed ones.

Weighted sums

Most decisions depend on several factors that matter by different amounts. A weighted sum multiplies each normalised factor by its importance and adds the results. Dividing by the total weight puts the answer back on the 0 to 1 scale.

score = (w1·v1 + w2·v2 + ... + wn·vn) / (w1 + w2 + ... + wn)

Only the ratio between weights matters. Weights of 2 and 1 behave exactly like 20 and 10.

From the book: deciding on dinner

Dave and Laurie choose between four dinners. Layer one merges their two preferences, with Dave's counted twice as much (2 to 1). Layer two merges that combined desire with price and time, weighted 2, 3 and 5 on a busy night. Time and price are stored inverted so that 1.0 is good (quick, cheap).

The two-layer dinner decision

All starting numbers are the book's. On a busy night burritos win with 8.0. Use the "relaxed evening" button for the book's second set of weights and steak wins with 4.2.

Layered models and compartmentalised confidence

The dinner example has three places where a change alters the answer: the individual tastes, the weights that merge them into desire, and the weights that merge desire with time and price. A change at the bottom travels all the way up. The book calls this propagation of change.

Layering is what keeps large models manageable, as long as you follow one discipline.

Compartmentalised confidence

Test each small piece by itself until you trust it. Then treat it as a black box when you build the next layer. If the dinner result looks wrong, you check one layer at a time, and you only change the layer where the fault is. Without this, every bad result sends you tuning twenty numbers at once.

Two habits make it work: keep every layer's inputs on the same scale (the book notes that scoring Dave 0 to 3 and Laurie 0 to 5 would quietly change the 2 to 1 ratio), and give each layer one job.

Weighted sum or multiplication?

The book's main combining tool is the weighted sum, but its big example in Part 5 also multiplies and divides factors. The difference matters when you move to the Infinite Axis system, which multiplies.

Add or multiply

Decision: shoot the enemy. Two factors, both 0 to 1. Drag ammo to zero and compare.

A weighted sum lets a strong factor make up for a weak one. That suits "which dinner". Multiplication lets any factor veto the option by going to zero. That suits "can I even do this". Part 6 uses the second.

Check yourself
  1. You weight health 3 and distance 1, but health is 0 to 100 and distance 0 to 1. What have you actually built? (Health dominates by roughly 300 to 1. Normalise first, then weight.)
  2. In the dinner example, why does Chinese take-out lose even though Laurie rates it 0.7? (Dave rates it 0.1 and his weight is double, so combined desire is only 0.3.)
  3. A result looks wrong in a three-layer model. What is the first step? (Check each layer's output alone to find which layer is wrong, before changing anything.)
