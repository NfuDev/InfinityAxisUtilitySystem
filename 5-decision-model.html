Part 5 · Chapters 14 to 16

Building a decision, changing it, and varying it

Chapter 14 assembles every tool so far into one complete decision. Chapter 15 asks when that decision should be reconsidered. Chapter 16 asks why the agent should not always take the top answer.

Decide what the decision is

The book treats a single decision as the atom of behaviour. Before scoring anything you choose how much goes inside that atom.

Its example: "which enemy do I attack" and "which weapon do I use" could be two decisions. Do them separately and you pick the most threatening enemy, then the best weapon in general. But one enemy may be weak against a weapon that is poor in general. Only by scoring every pair (enemy, weapon) as one option do you find that. This is the Prisoner's Dilemma lesson from Part 1: optimising one choice in isolation gives an acceptable answer, not the best one.

Rule of thumb from the book

Group choices into one decision when they strongly depend on each other. Keep them apart when they do not, because every grouping multiplies the number of options (3 enemies × 4 weapons = 12).

Steps 2 and 3: relationships and connections

// Relationship: how long to bring a health pool to zero
double TimeToKill(double health, double damage, double accuracy) {
    double dps = accuracy * damage;
    return dps != 0 ? health / dps : 1000;      // 1000 = "effectively never"
}

timeToKillEnemy = TimeToKill(enemy.health, myWeapon.Damage(dist),    myWeapon.Accuracy(dist));
timeToKillMe    = TimeToKill(my.health,    enemy.weapon.Damage(dist), enemy.weapon.Accuracy(dist, enemy.accuracyModifier));

// Connection 1: a weighted mean. Killing him fast is 3x as important as surviving long.
threatRatio = (timeToKillMe + 3 * timeToKillEnemy) / 4;        // lower = attack this one first

// Connection 2: how close is he to the objective (a detonator)?
urgency = max(0, 1 - pow(distToGoal, 0.4) / 10);               // 1 next to it, 0 far away

// Final score. Lower is better.
score = threatRatio / urgency;

Notice the three different combining methods in a dozen lines: a product (accuracy × damage), a weighted mean (threat ratio), and a division (so that rising urgency pulls the score down sharply). The book picks each one by asking how the two inputs should trade off, then checks a few sample rows by hand.

The 1,000-second clamp is deliberate

A weapon that cannot reach the target has zero damage per second, which would divide by zero. Clamping to 1,000 gives that pair an enormous score, so it sorts to the bottom without any special case. Chapter 16 relies on this later.

The moral the book draws

You cannot decide without information. Information is ambiguous until you relate it to something else. Small decisions can be combined into larger ones. And then: information rarely stays the same for long, which is the next chapter.

Changing a decision (chapter 15)

How often to think

ApproachHow it worksProblem
Every frameRescore everything continuously.Wasteful, and faster than any human reacts. The book cites about 240 ms as a typical human reaction time, roughly 12 frames.
Fixed periodRescore every N milliseconds. Stagger agents across frames so only a fraction think each frame.Can miss sudden events between ticks.
PollingCheck whether inputs changed, and rescore only then.The checking itself is expensive, and you must remember old values.
Event-drivenThe thing that changed broadcasts it. A thrown grenade tells nearby agents.Brittle alone: agents stand idle if no event arrives, and message order matters.
The book's recommendation: a hybrid

Give each kind of information its own timer, sized to how fast it really changes, and let events interrupt. For the Dudes: rescore after each shot (the natural smallest unit of action), and let "a rocket is coming at me" interrupt at once. Between choosing and firing, keep asking one cheap question: can I still do this? If the target died or went behind cover, drop the plan immediately.

Strobing and decision momentum

The book's "Flotilla of Futility": an AI fleet sails toward an undefended city. The player moves troops in just before it arrives, leaving another city empty. The fleet turns around. The player moves the troops back. The fleet sails back and forth forever and never fires a shot.

The AI rescored correctly every time. What it lacked was any cost for abandoning what it was already doing. The fix is decision momentum: new information has to beat not only the old score but the commitment already made.

Strobing against momentum

Two targets with nearly equal, noisy utility. The coloured strip under the plot shows which one the agent is attacking at each moment.

With no momentum the agent switches dozens of times and achieves nothing. A small bonus removes almost all of it while still allowing a switch when one option becomes clearly better. Thinking less often helps too, but it only hides strobing; momentum removes the cause.

Three more factors that create momentum naturally

  • Time commitment. Include the cost of getting there. The fleet's score for a city should fall with travel time, and time already spent sailing is part of the cost of turning round.
  • Information expiry. Facts about things you do not control go stale. Scale your confidence in "that city is undefended" by how long it will take to act on it. Far-away opportunities should be trusted less. The book warns not to overdo this, or the fleet never leaves port.
  • The value of finishing. Use increasing marginal utility near completion. An enemy at 10% health is worth finishing before switching, and a building at 90% is worth completing. The book suggests running time-to-kill through a curve so that short remaining times count for much more, which is a response curve applied to change the shape of a factor's influence, not just its weight.

Variation in choice (chapter 16)

The chapter opens with a bank robbery in which all 20 customers gasp, drop and scream in perfect unison, and do exactly the same thing when you come back an hour later. Each reaction was reasonable. All of them choosing it together, every time, is what makes it absurd.

Two kinds of variation are needed: between different agents in the same situation, and for one agent across repeated situations. Modelling each character's life history to get it would be impossible, so the book uses the score list you already have plus structured randomness.

MethodHowVerdict in the book
Best onlySort, take the first.Predictable. Every agent does the same thing.
Random from top nTake the best 25% (a percentage adapts as options come and go), pick one uniformly.Better, but treats the 1st and the 8th as equally good even when they are not.
Weighted random from top nSame cut-off, but each option's chance is proportional to how good its score is.Good. Still needs an arbitrary cut-off.
Weighted random from allNo cut-off. Bad options get tiny buckets and almost never occur.Preferred. No sort needed, no magic number, and one agent in a hundred does something surprising.

Because the Dudes score is "lower is better", the book turns scores into bucket widths by dividing the total by each score, which also scales the weights automatically:

weight[i] = round( sum of all scores / score[i] )     then build edges and throw a ball, as in Part 3

Run 1,000 agents through the same situation

Uses whatever is currently in the Dudes simulator above (preset 4 has the most options). Each run sends 1,000 agents and shows what share chose each pair.

Why allow the odd poor choice?

With the full weighted list, roughly four in five agents pick one of the dozen sensible options and are spread between them. A few pick something mediocre. The book argues this is a feature: a crowd where a couple of individuals behave unexpectedly reads as a crowd of individuals, and a player has to pay attention to which enemy is doing what.

One requirement before you can do this

Scores must be on a scale where ratios mean something. "Twice the score" has to mean roughly "twice as good". The book's advice is to design the scoring with this in mind from the start ("measure twice, cut once"), which is one more reason to keep everything normalised.
